%% ── ABSTRACT ────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
Deep neural networks tend to make overconfident predictions
on inputs that lie far outside their training distribution,
a serious problem in safety-critical applications.
We address out-of-distribution (OOD) detection by shifting
the energy-based scoring function from a discriminative
classifier into the discrete latent space of a
Vector-Quantized Variational Autoencoder (VQ-VAE) trained
exclusively on in-distribution data.
An unsupervised energy model on VQ-VAE latent codes with a
contrastive hinge loss requires no OOD labels.
A supervised MLP in the same space provides an upper-bound
reference.
On CIFAR-10 (in-distribution), CIFAR-100 (near-OOD), and
SVHN (far-OOD), our Latent MLP achieves AUROC~0.980 on
SVHN---a 4.6-point improvement over the energy baseline---
while reducing FPR@95 from 25.8\% to 10.0\%.
On the harder near-OOD benchmark the energy baseline leads
by 15 AUROC points, pointing to the need for encoders that
capture semantic rather than purely visual structure.
Code: \url{https://github.com/harshitjain25/csc8851-vqvae-ood}.
\end{abstract}

%% ── 1. INTRODUCTION ─────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}

Consider a medical imaging system trained to classify ten
common conditions.
When it encounters a rare disease it has never seen, the
desired behaviour is to flag uncertainty.
Instead, modern deep neural networks regularly output
high-confidence predictions for inputs entirely outside
their training distribution~\cite{nguyen2015}.
This overconfidence is not a quirk---it is systematic,
arising because these models optimise for discriminating
among known categories, not for recognising what they do not
know.
In autonomous driving, industrial inspection, and
healthcare, deploying such a model without reliable OOD
detection is a real safety risk.

\textbf{Problem formulation.}
Let $p_{\mathrm{in}}(x)$ be the training distribution.
At inference we must decide whether input $x$ came from
$p_{\mathrm{in}}$ or an unknown $p_{\mathrm{out}}$.
The standard approach is a scoring function
$s:\mathcal{X}\!\to\!\mathbb{R}$ and threshold $\tau$:
\begin{equation}
  g(x;\tau) = \mathbf{1}[s(x)>\tau].   \label{eq:decision}
\end{equation}
A good score maximises the area under the ROC curve
regardless of where the threshold is placed.

\textbf{Limitations of existing methods.}
The maximum softmax probability~\cite{hendrycks2017} works
surprisingly well on easy cases, but softmax normalisation
forces probability mass onto a known class even for
unfamiliar inputs.
Liu~\etal~\cite{liu2020} proposed using the free energy of
the classifier,
\begin{equation}
  E(x;f) = -T\cdot\log\!\sum_{i=1}^{K} e^{f_i(x)/T},
  \label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
which correlates with the log-partition function of the
data distribution and avoids the overconfidence bias of
softmax.
Despite this advantage, the energy still comes from a
classifier trained for class separation---it answers
``which class does this look like?'' rather than ``does
this look like \emph{anything} from the training set?''

\textbf{Our approach.}
We ask whether a generative latent space better captures
distributional familiarity.
A VQ-VAE~\cite{vqvae2017} trained only on CIFAR-10 builds
a discrete visual vocabulary shaped entirely by
reconstruction quality.
When an OOD image passes through this encoder, the
resulting code sequence is constructed from a vocabulary
never designed for it, producing an ``awkward'' latent
representation that can be detected---without ever seeing
labeled OOD examples.
We evaluate on both near-OOD (CIFAR-100, visually similar
to CIFAR-10) and far-OOD (SVHN, structurally different),
probing both ends of the difficulty spectrum.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
  \item We faithfully reproduce the WRN-40-2 energy
        detector of Liu~\etal~\cite{liu2020}, confirming
        their numbers as a rigorous baseline.
  \item We train an unsupervised latent energy model on
        VQ-VAE embeddings via a contrastive hinge loss,
        with no OOD labels at training time.
  \item We train a supervised latent MLP as an upper-bound
        reference and explicitly identify its data-leakage
        limitation.
  \item We show that a grid-searched blend weight
        $\alpha^*$ automatically discards a near-random
        signal, recovering the performance of the stronger
        detector alone.
\end{enumerate}

%% ── 2. RELATED WORK ─────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Softmax confidence.}
Hendrycks and Gimpel~\cite{hendrycks2017} established the
maximum softmax probability as the standard OOD baseline.
It requires no model modification and works on far-OOD
data, but the normalisation constraint means the model
can never truly express ignorance.

\textbf{ODIN.}
Liang~\etal~\cite{liang2018} improved detection by applying
temperature scaling and a small adversarial perturbation to
the input, widening the in/OOD score gap considerably.
Both hyperparameters must be re-tuned for each OOD dataset,
which limits deployment flexibility.

\textbf{Mahalanobis distance.}
Lee~\etal~\cite{lee2018} fit class-conditional Gaussians to
penultimate-layer features and use Mahalanobis distance to
the nearest class centre as an OOD score.
This is effective but requires storing a full covariance
matrix per class and assumes Gaussian feature distributions.

\textbf{Outlier Exposure.}
Hendrycks~\etal~\cite{hendrycks2019} showed that fine-tuning
a classifier on auxiliary OOD data---encouraging uniform
predictions on it---improves detection significantly.
Our supervised latent MLP applies the same idea in VQ-VAE
latent space rather than pixel space, and inherits the same
core vulnerability: performance degrades whenever the
auxiliary data fails to represent real test OOD distributions.

\textbf{Energy-based OOD.}
Liu~\etal~\cite{liu2020} formalised Eq.~\eqref{eq:energy}
by showing its connection to the log-partition function of a
Gibbs distribution, giving it stronger theoretical grounding
than softmax.
Our work preserves this energy formulation but relocates the
scoring function from a discriminative classifier into a
generative latent space.

\textbf{VQ-VAE.}
Van~den~Oord~\etal~\cite{vqvae2017} introduced the
Vector-Quantized VAE, which encodes images as sequences of
discrete tokens from a learned codebook.
Because the codebook is shaped purely by reconstruction
on in-distribution data, it provides a familiarity signal
that is orthogonal to any class labels---a property we
exploit for OOD detection.

\subsection{VQ-VAE Latent Encoding}

The VQ-VAE (trained by Parsh Jadon) has encoder $E_\theta$,
codebook $\mathcal{C}=\{\mathbf{e}_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$
($K\!=\!512$), and decoder $D_\phi$.
Two stride-2 convolutional layers compress each
$32{\times}32$ image to an $8{\times}8{\times}64$ grid;
each position is then snapped to the nearest codebook entry:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{z}_q = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\mathbf{e}_k\in\mathcal{C}}
    \|\mathbf{z}_e-\mathbf{e}_k\|_2,   \label{eq:quant}
\end{equation}
with $\mathbf{z}_e\!=\!E_\theta(x)$.
The straight-through estimator handles gradients through
the non-differentiable argmin, enabling end-to-end
training via:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{vq}} =
  \underbrace{\|x{-}D_\phi(\mathbf{z}_q)\|^2}_{\text{recon.}}
  +\underbrace{\|\mathrm{sg}[\mathbf{z}_e]{-}\mathbf{e}_k\|^2}_{\text{codebook}}
  +\beta\underbrace{\|\mathbf{z}_e{-}\mathrm{sg}[\mathbf{e}_k]\|^2}_{\text{commit.}},
  \label{eq:vqloss}
\end{equation}
where $\beta\!=\!0.25$.
Training uses Adam (lr\,$=\!10^{-3}$), batch size 128,
10 epochs on CIFAR-10 only.
Flattening yields $\mathbf{z}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{4096}$.

\subsection{Latent Energy Model}

$E_{\mathrm{lat}}$ is a three-layer MLP (256 hidden units,
ReLU) mapping $\mathbb{R}^{4096}\!\to\!\mathbb{R}$, trained
only on CIFAR-10 latents.
Gaussian noise $\mathbf{z}_n\!\sim\!\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$
serves as dataset-agnostic pseudo-OOD, requiring no real
OOD data collection.
A squared hinge loss with margins $m_{\mathrm{in}}\!=\!-10$
and $m_{\mathrm{out}}\!=\!-5$ enforces separation:
\begin{align}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{in}} &=
    \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim p_\mathrm{ID}}\!
    [\max(0,E_{\mathrm{lat}}(\mathbf{z})-m_{\mathrm{in}})^2],
    \label{eq:lin}\\
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}} &=
    \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}}\!
    [\max(0,m_{\mathrm{out}}-E_{\mathrm{lat}}(\mathbf{z}))^2],
    \label{eq:lout}
\end{align}
with $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{energy}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{in}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{out}}$.
The hinge zeroes the gradient once a sample is on the
correct side of its margin, preventing energy collapse.
Training uses Adam (lr\,$=\!10^{-3}$) for 10 epochs.

\subsection{Supervised OOD MLP}

To establish an upper-bound reference, we train a binary
MLP (same 3-layer, 256-unit architecture) with binary
cross-entropy:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MLP}} = -\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{z},y)}\!
  [y\log\hat{y}+(1{-}y)\log(1{-}\hat{y})],
  \label{eq:bce}
\end{equation}
where $y\!=\!0$ for CIFAR-10 and $y\!=\!1$ for CIFAR-100 /
SVHN latents.

\textbf{Data-leakage caveat.}
After evaluation we found this formulation constitutes
\emph{data leakage}: the MLP is trained on labels from the
exact datasets used for testing.
The MLP numbers should be read as an upper bound, not a
deployable result.
A sound protocol would train on a disjoint auxiliary OOD
source and evaluate zero-shot.
We document this explicitly because it affects how
Table~\ref{tab:results} should be interpreted.

\subsection{Combined Score and Threshold}

Both scores are min-max normalised to $[0,1]$ using
CIFAR-10 statistics:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{s} = (s-s_{\min})\,/\,(s_{\max}-s_{\min}),
  \label{eq:norm}
\end{equation}
then blended as a weighted average:
\begin{equation}
  s_{\mathrm{comb}}(\mathbf{z}) =
    \alpha\,\tilde{E}_{\mathrm{lat}}(\mathbf{z})
    +(1{-}\alpha)\,\tilde{y}(\mathbf{z}),
  \label{eq:comb}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha^*$ grid-searched over $\{0.0,\ldots,1.0\}$
on a held-out 50\% validation split and applied to the
remaining 50\%.
The threshold $\tau$ is fixed so that TPR\,=\,0.95 on the
CIFAR-10 test set.

\subsection{Implementation Challenges}

\textbf{Memory-mapped OOD data.}
Loading 300K Random Images~\cite{hendrycks2019} as a single
PyTorch tensor ($\approx$3.5\,GB) crashed our Colab session
during fine-tuning.
We wrote a custom \texttt{RandomImagesDataset} using NumPy
memory-mapped I/O so only the current mini-batch loads into
RAM, cutting peak usage to near zero.

\textbf{Baseline model choice.}
We initially compared against a small two-layer CNN.
The gains looked impressive but were mostly an artefact of
a weak baseline.
Switching to WRN-40-2 and training for 100 epochs with
cosine LR decay---until we matched Liu~\etal's reported
numbers---enabled a fair comparison.

\textbf{Weak-signal contamination.}
A naive $\alpha\!=\!0.5$ blend of the 0.51-AUROC energy
model and the 0.98-AUROC MLP pulled the combined score to
0.94.
Grid-searching $\alpha$ on the validation split returned
$\alpha^*\!=\!0.0$, confirming the noisy signal should be
discarded entirely rather than averaged in.

%% ── 4. EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS ──────────────────────────────
\section{Experiments and Results}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\textbf{Datasets.}
CIFAR-10~\cite{krizhevsky2009} (50\,000 train / 10\,000
test, 10 categories) serves as in-distribution.
Near-OOD is CIFAR-100~\cite{krizhevsky2009} (10\,000 test
images, 100 categories): identical resolution and visual
style to CIFAR-10 make semantic distinction very difficult.
Far-OOD is SVHN~\cite{netzer2011} (26\,032 street-view
digit photographs): structurally unlike natural objects,
this is the easier benchmark.
Together they probe both ends of the difficulty spectrum.

\textbf{Metrics.}
Following~\cite{liu2020,hendrycks2017}: AUROC ($\uparrow$)
measures overall separability; AUPR ($\uparrow$) captures
precision-recall trade-offs; FPR@95TPR ($\downarrow$)
gives the operational false-alarm rate at 95\% recall.

\textbf{Baselines.}
Two WRN-40-2 variants from~\cite{liu2020}: pre-trained
energy (no fine-tuning) and energy fine-tuned on 300K
Random Images.
Both trained on CIFAR-10 for 100 epochs, SGD, cosine LR,
batch size 128.

\textbf{Baseline reproduction.}
Our reproduced WRN-40-2 scores---AUROC 0.934 on SVHN and
0.859 on CIFAR-100---match~\cite{liu2020} closely,
confirming implementation correctness and fair comparison.

\textbf{Far-OOD (SVHN).}
The Latent MLP achieves AUROC 0.980 and FPR@95 10.0\%, a
4.6-point AUROC gain and 15.8-point false-alarm reduction
over the pre-trained energy baseline.
SVHN digit images are structurally alien to CIFAR-10 natural
objects; the VQ-VAE codebook---built from natural scene
patches---produces clearly anomalous codes for digits, and
the MLP exploits this effectively.

\textbf{Near-OOD (CIFAR-100).}
The energy baseline leads decisively: 0.859 vs.\ 0.704
AUROC.
CIFAR-100 shares CIFAR-10's resolution, colour palette, and
subject matter; the VQ-VAE codebook reconstructs both
equally well because they share the same natural-image
textures.
The discriminative WRN-40-2, with 40 supervised layers,
captures the semantic difference between the two
datasets---something the reconstruction-driven encoder
simply cannot.
This reveals a fundamental trade-off: generative features
capture visual fidelity while discriminative features
capture semantic membership, and near-OOD detection demands
the latter.

\textbf{Effect of fine-tuning.}
Fine-tuning WRN-40-2 on 300K random internet images
\emph{degraded} AUROC by $-$15 points on SVHN and $-$12
on CIFAR-100.
The auxiliary proxy (random internet crops) does not
resemble SVHN digits or CIFAR-100 nature photos; the model
learns to flag random texture specifically, contaminating
the boundary for real OOD sets.
This reinforces a known limitation of Outlier Exposure:
the choice of auxiliary data is critical.

\textbf{Latent energy failure.}
The Latent Energy model scores AUROC 0.511--0.533, near
random.
The root cause is a mismatch: training separates CIFAR-10
latents from Gaussian noise, but real OOD latents (SVHN,
CIFAR-100) look nothing like Gaussian noise in VQ-VAE
space.
Pseudo-OOD quality is not a minor detail---the training
negatives must inhabit the same latent regions as real OOD
data.

\textbf{Combined score ($\alpha^*$).}
Grid search returns $\alpha^*\!=\!0.0$ on both datasets,
reducing the combination to the MLP alone.
This is informative rather than a failure: it confirms the
energy signal adds no information beyond the MLP.
At $\alpha\!=\!0.5$, the 0.51-AUROC energy score dilutes
the 0.98-AUROC MLP to $\approx$0.94; the grid search
correctly discards the weak signal.

\textbf{Overall picture.}
Taken together, the results reveal a consistent pattern.
Detectors that rely on visual texture (our VQ-VAE latent
space) succeed when OOD data is structurally distinct but
fail when it shares the same texture distribution as
in-distribution data.
Detectors that rely on semantic class features (WRN-40-2
energy) capture fine-grained semantic distinctions but are
sensitive to the quality of any auxiliary data used during
fine-tuning.
The gap between near-OOD and far-OOD performance is almost
identical across all five methods, suggesting this is a
fundamental property of the evaluation benchmarks rather
than a shortcoming of any single detector.
An ideal system would pair a semantically rich encoder with
an energy function that captures distributional familiarity
at multiple levels of abstraction.
Our experiments suggest the primary bottleneck is the
encoder architecture itself---not the scoring function
placed on top---which directly motivates the WideResNet-backed
VQ-VAE direction we outline in the conclusions.

%% ── 5. CONCLUSIONS ──────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This project asked whether a generative latent space can
improve OOD detection over a discriminative energy score.
The answer is dataset-dependent.
For far-OOD data (SVHN), the VQ-VAE latent space clearly
helps: our Latent MLP surpasses the energy baseline by
4.6 AUROC points and halves FPR@95.
For near-OOD data (CIFAR-100), the discriminative baseline
wins by 15 AUROC points, because semantic features capture
what the generative model cannot.

Three concrete lessons emerged from this work.

\textbf{Lesson 1: Avoid data leakage in supervised detectors.}
The supervised OOD MLP is trained on labels drawn from the
exact datasets used for testing.
A sound protocol would collect a small labeled set from a
\emph{disjoint} auxiliary source---say, 50 examples from
the Textures or Places-365 dataset---and evaluate zero-shot
on CIFAR-100 and SVHN.
Studying how performance degrades as the auxiliary set
shrinks would also quantify how much supervision the latent
space actually needs.

\textbf{Lesson 2: Pseudo-OOD quality determines energy model quality.}
Gaussian noise in $\mathbb{R}^{4096}$ occupies a region
completely unlike any real image latent code; it is a
trivially easy negative that teaches the model nothing
about the actual OOD boundary.
Harder negatives---codes produced by CutMix-style latent
interpolation, codebook-index shuffling, or cross-class
code swaps---would force the energy model to learn a
tighter boundary and likely close the gap with the
supervised MLP.

\textbf{Lesson 3: Validate blend weights before combining detectors.}
Mixing a near-random signal with a strong one actively
hurts performance.
A principled alternative is to gate on signal quality:
only include a detector in the ensemble if its held-out
validation AUROC exceeds a minimum threshold, or learn
$\alpha$ end-to-end rather than by post-hoc grid search.

Looking ahead, the most direct path to closing the near-OOD
gap is a semantically richer encoder.
A WideResNet-backed VQ-VAE would learn latent codes shaped
by high-level class structure rather than low-level pixel
texture, potentially combining the discriminative advantages
of WRN-40-2 with the generative advantages of the codebook
vocabulary.
Alternatively, training the VQ-VAE with a joint
reconstruction-plus-OOD objective could produce a codebook
that is inherently more sensitive to distributional shift.
