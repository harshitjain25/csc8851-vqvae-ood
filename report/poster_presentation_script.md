# Poster Presentation Script
## For First-Time Poster Presenters

---

## Before You Start: How Poster Sessions Work

A poster session is very different from a regular talk. Here's what to expect:

- People will walk up to your poster at random times — sometimes mid-explanation, sometimes just as you finish.
- You don't read the poster out loud. You use the poster as a visual prop while you **talk naturally**.
- Most people will give you 3–5 minutes. Some will be very interested and stay for 10–15 minutes. Some will glance and walk away. **All of this is normal.**
- Your job is to tell a story: Why does this problem matter? What did you do? What happened? What did you learn?
- Have a **60-second version** and a **5-minute version** ready. Start short and let people ask for more.

**Body language tips:**
- Stand next to the poster, slightly to the side so people can see it.
- Point at sections as you mention them — it keeps the listener's eye on the relevant part.
- Smile. It's a conversation, not an exam.

## The 60-Second Elevator Pitch

*Use this when someone walks up and you're not sure how interested they are.*

> "So our project is about a problem called Out-of-Distribution detection. The short version is: AI models are dangerously overconfident — you can show a trained image classifier a picture of a pizza and it'll say 'that's a dog' with 98% confidence. We built a system to detect when a model sees something it was never trained on. We took a published method from NeurIPS 2020 as our baseline, reproduced it, and then extended it using a completely different kind of model — a generative model called VQ-VAE. Our best result beats the baseline by 5 percentage points on one of the two datasets."

Then pause and see if they want more. If they nod or ask "what's a VQ-VAE?" — that's your cue to go deeper.

## The Full 5-Minute Walkthrough

### Block 1: Start with the Problem (Point to Section 1 — Motivation)

> "Let me start with the problem. You train an AI on CIFAR-10 — 10 categories like cats, dogs, and cars. The model gets good — maybe 94% accuracy. Now you show it a picture it has never seen — say, a street view digit or a chest X-ray. Instead of saying 'I don't know,' it picks one of its 10 classes and says it with high confidence. That's the overconfidence problem."
>
> "This is fine for a toy dataset. But in healthcare or self-driving cars, a model confidently wrong is dangerous."
>
> "Out-of-distribution detection is the task of building a score that goes high when the input is foreign, and low when it's something the model knows."

---

### Block 2: The Baseline We Reproduced (Point to Section 2 and Section 3)

> "There's a paper from NeurIPS 2020 by Liu et al. that proposed a smart solution called the Energy Score. Instead of looking at the softmax probabilities — which are notoriously overconfident — you compute a single number from the raw logits called the energy. In-distribution images get low energy, OOD images get high energy."
>
> "They used a big, powerful classifier called WideResNet-40-2. Our first job was to reproduce their exact numbers — and we did. We got AUROC 0.93 on SVHN and 0.86 on CIFAR-100, which matches the paper."

*(Point to the results table in Section 7 — the WRN-40-2 rows.)*

> "So that's our baseline. Now, what did we do differently?"

---

### Block 3: Our Idea — VQ-VAE (Point to Section 4 — Pipeline diagram)

> "Here's our key idea. The original method asks 'does this image fit one of my 10 classes?' We want to ask a different question: 'does this image look like a CIFAR-10 image at all?' That's a question about visual structure, not class labels."
>
> "To answer it, we use a VQ-VAE — which is a type of generative model. Instead of classifying images, it learns to compress and reconstruct them. It learns a vocabulary of 512 visual code words. When you give it an image, it turns that image into a sequence of codes."

*(Point to the pipeline diagram — the VQ-VAE box.)*

> "We trained the VQ-VAE only on CIFAR-10. So its vocabulary was built entirely from CIFAR-10 images. When you give it an SVHN digit or a CIFAR-100 image, it has to force-fit that unfamiliar image into a vocabulary that wasn't made for it. The resulting code pattern is 'awkward' — and that's what we detect."

---

### Block 4: What We Built on Top (Point to Sections 5 and 5b)

> "We built two detectors that both take this 4096-dimensional code as input."
>
> "The first is an Energy MLP — an unsupervised model that uses a contrastive loss. We push real CIFAR-10 codes to low energy and fake 'pseudo-OOD' codes to high energy. The pseudo-OOD is generated by scrambling CIFAR-10 codes — like shuffling the words in a sentence. We don't need any real OOD data."
>
> "The second is an OOD MLP — a supervised classifier that's trained to output a probability between 0 and 1 for whether the code is in-distribution or not."
>
> "We then blend the two scores together."

---

### Block 5: The Results (Point to Sections 7, 7a, 7b)

> "Here are the numbers."

*(Point to the results table.)*

> "On the easy test — SVHN, which is very different from CIFAR-10 — our Latent MLP gets 0.98 AUROC versus the baseline's 0.93. That's a 5-point improvement, and the false alarm rate drops from 26% to 10%. So our VQ-VAE approach clearly wins here."
>
> "On the hard test — CIFAR-100, which looks very similar to CIFAR-10 — the baseline wins. 0.86 versus our 0.70. The big classifier has more capacity to tell apart fine-grained visual differences."

*(Point to the bar chart and ROC curves.)*

> "These graphs show the same thing visually. The solid bars are the Far-OOD results — you can see our green bar is the tallest. The hatched bars are Near-OOD — the blue baseline is still ahead there."

---

### Block 6: What Didn't Work (Point to Section 8 and 9)

> "Let me tell you about some things that didn't work — because that's where the real learning happened."
>
> "First: energy fine-tuning actually made the baseline worse. We fine-tuned WRN-40-2 with extra loss on 300,000 random training images, expecting it to improve. Instead, the AUROC dropped from 0.93 to 0.78. Why? Because those random training images don't look like real OOD images. The model learned to flag random images specifically, but that hurt its ability to flag actual OOD distributions."
>
> "Second: our Energy MLP got near-random performance — AUROC 0.51. Barely better than flipping a coin. The reason is our fake OOD training data — scrambled CIFAR-10 codes — doesn't look like real CIFAR-100 codes. We taught the model the wrong boundary."
>
> "Third: when we blended the energy and MLP scores equally, performance got worse than using just the MLP. We were averaging a good signal with a near-random one. The fix was to grid-search the blend weight — and the optimal weight turned out to be zero for the energy signal. The model correctly learned to ignore it."

---

### Block 7: What We'd Do Differently (Point to Section 10)

> "If we continued this work, the main improvements would be:"
>
> "One — smarter fake OOD. Instead of random shuffles and Gaussian noise, we'd create more realistic 'hard negatives' using techniques like CutMix."
>
> "Two — larger codebook. Right now we have 512 code words. With more words, the vocabulary is richer and can better distinguish CIFAR-10 codes from CIFAR-100 codes."
>
> "Three — a stronger encoder. Using a WideResNet inside the VQ-VAE would give it more powerful features to work with."

## Handling Common Questions

**Q: "What is AUROC exactly?"**
> "It measures how well a score separates two groups — in-distribution vs out-of-distribution images. 0.5 means completely random, 1.0 means perfect separation. Think of it as: if you randomly pick one ID image and one OOD image, AUROC is the probability your score ranks the OOD image higher."

**Q: "Why VQ-VAE specifically?"**
> "We wanted a generative model — something that learns the data distribution rather than just class labels. VQ-VAE is a good choice because it produces discrete codes, which are easy to manipulate and interpret. The quantization step forces the encoder to use a fixed vocabulary, which is what creates the 'awkward code' effect for OOD images."

**Q: "Why did you use CIFAR-10 as in-distribution?"**
> "It's the standard benchmark for OOD detection research. Almost every paper in this space uses CIFAR-10 as the ID dataset, CIFAR-100 as Near-OOD, and SVHN as Far-OOD. Using the same benchmark makes our results directly comparable to the paper we're reproducing."

**Q: "Could this work in practice / real world?"**
> "In principle yes — the VQ-VAE pipeline is fast at inference time and doesn't require a large classifier. The challenge is that it still struggles on Near-OOD, which is the hard case that actually matters in practice. We'd need the improvements we mentioned to make it production-ready."

**Q: "What's the difference between Near-OOD and Far-OOD?"**
> "Far-OOD images look very different from your training data — SVHN digits look nothing like CIFAR-10 objects, so almost any detector works. Near-OOD images look similar to your training data — CIFAR-100 has many of the same types of objects as CIFAR-10, just more of them. Near-OOD is the realistic, hard version of the problem."

**Q: "Did you use any pre-trained models?"**
> "For the VQ-VAE, Energy MLP, and OOD MLP — no, we trained everything from scratch on CIFAR-10. For the WRN-40-2 baseline, we also trained it from scratch following the same setup as the original paper."

## If Someone Seems Very Technical

Add this detail about the energy score:

> "The energy is mathematically related to the log-partition function in probabilistic models. Liu et al. showed that unlike the softmax probability — which is a ratio and saturates — the energy has better theoretical alignment with the data distribution. Specifically, e^(-E(x)/T) is proportional to the joint density p(x, y) under certain assumptions, so low-energy regions correspond to high-density regions of the training distribution."

## Final Tip

The best poster presentations feel like a **conversation**, not a lecture. If someone asks a question mid-explanation — great, answer it and come back. If someone says "I only have 2 minutes" — great, give them the 60-second pitch. The poster is there to support you, not the other way around. You know this project better than anyone in that room. Just talk about what you did.
